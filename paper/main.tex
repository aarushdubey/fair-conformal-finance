\begin{abstract}
Conformal prediction has gained widespread adoption as an uncertainty quantification tool because it provides finite-sample, distribution-free guarantees without imposing parametric assumptions on data distributions. In retail credit scoring and fraud monitoring, however, extending these guarantees to protected demographic groups produces an unexpected dilemma. When calibration algorithms enforce exact coverage parity across groups that have different levels of epistemic uncertainty, the prediction sets for the noisier or historically underrepresented group widen substantially. Rather than receiving a decisive decision, applicants from protected backgrounds disproportionately receive ambiguous prediction sets containing both approve and reject labels. Within operational banking pipelines, such ambiguous outputs cannot proceed through automated clearing; they trigger manual document audits, lengthy verification queues, and elevated customer abandonment. We term this dynamic the \emph{coverage-equity paradox}: enforcing equalized error rates in the predictor simply transfers the disadvantage into operational delays. In this paper, we propose \textbf{FairTransCP}, a group-aware calibration framework that balances coverage equity against set-size parity by interpolating group-specific quantiles with a pooled marginal cutoff. We prove a finite-sample lower bound on subgroup coverage under this regularized formulation. Across three financial and demographic benchmarks (German Credit, Taiwan Default, and Adult Census) evaluated on Random Forests, XGBoost, and LightGBM, FairTransCP curbs set-size disparity by up to 59.8\% while maintaining empirical coverage above nominal 90\% thresholds.
\end{abstract}

\section{INTRODUCTION}

Automated scoring algorithms evaluate millions of retail loan applications, credit card issuances, and transaction audits every day \citep{barocas2023fairness}. Unlike search engines or recommender systems, financial screening operates under strict regulatory oversight. When an underwriting pipeline evaluates a borderline applicant, point-estimate probabilities alone provide insufficient protection against risk. A raw model score of 0.52 indicates marginal creditworthiness, but it fails to communicate whether that estimate is statistically robust or merely an artifact of sample sparsity. Underwriters need reliable uncertainty intervals before taking adverse action or approving capital disbursements.

Conformal prediction (CP), pioneered by \citet{vovk2005algorithmic} and extended to split-sample configurations by \citet{lei2018distribution} and \citet{romano2020classification}, provides a principled response to this challenge. Assuming only that calibration and test observations are exchangeable, conformal methods construct prediction sets $C(X) \subseteq \mathcal{Y}$ with finite-sample, distribution-free validity. At a user-chosen error tolerance $\alpha \in (0, 1)$, a split conformal classifier guarantees marginal coverage across the population:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:marginal_guarantee}
\mathbb{P}\left(Y \in C(X)\right) \ge 1 - \alpha.
\end{equation}

Although Equation~(\ref{eq:marginal_guarantee}) provides rigorous statistical protection for the average applicant, it does not guarantee fairness across sub-populations. A predictor could achieve nominal 90\% coverage across the pooled population while covering a majority demographic at 96\% and an underserved minority demographic at only 76\%. Such discrepancies violate both moral fairness and fair lending laws.

To address this gap, researchers introduced group-conditional conformal prediction \citep{romano2020classification}, which computes dedicated calibration quantiles for each protected demographic stratum $a \in \mathcal{A}$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:group_guarantee}
\mathbb{P}\left(Y \in C(X) \mid A = a\right) \ge 1 - \alpha \quad \forall a \in \mathcal{A}.
\end{equation}

While Equation~(\ref{eq:group_guarantee}) satisfies formal parity in mathematical notation, its operational impact in real-world lending is deeply problematic. In retail credit, decisions are binary: an applicant is approved ($Y=1$) or declined ($Y=0$). Disadvantaged applicant groups often exhibit higher label noise or thinner credit files, leading to wider posterior variance in the base model. To guarantee nominal coverage on a noisier group, the calibration algorithm must push that group's quantile cutoff substantially upward. As a direct consequence, the predictor frequently outputs ambiguous two-element sets:
\begin{equation}
C(X) = \{\text{approve}, \text{reject}\}.
\end{equation}

In modern consumer banking, an ambiguous prediction set is far from benign. Under straight-through processing (STP) pipelines, decisive predictions ({approve} or {reject}) are cleared automatically in seconds. When an applicant receives the ambiguous set $\{\text{approve}, \text{reject}\}$, the automated workflow halts. The application is diverted to secondary underwriting, where human auditors demand paystubs, tax filings, and employment verifications \citep{charpentier2024insurance, cresswell2025conformal}. Borrowers subjected to multi-week review queues frequently abandon their applications, often turning to high-cost alternatives. Furthermore, subjective human auditing historically disfavors minority applicants who lack traditional collateral.

We designate this phenomenon the \textbf{coverage-equity paradox}: \emph{rigidly enforcing demographic coverage parity shifts the penalty from classification error into administrative delays, leaving protected applicants worse off in practice.}

\paragraph{Our Contributions.}
To resolve this tension, we introduce \textbf{FairTransCP} (\textbf{Fair} Financial \textbf{Trans}action \textbf{C}onformal \textbf{P}rediction). The core contributions of this work are:
First, we formalize the mechanism of the coverage-equity paradox, proving that the width of the ambiguous decision region grows monotonically with group calibration quantiles. Across nine experimental settings spanning German Credit, Taiwan Default, and Adult Census datasets, we document that standard group-conditional calibration inflates set-size disparity by up to 59.8\%.
Second, we formulate FairTransCP as a regularized calibration scheme that interpolates between marginal and group-conditional cutoffs through parameter $\lambda \in [0, 1]$. We prove a finite-sample lower bound showing that subgroup coverage degradation is strictly bounded by the probability mass between the group and pooled quantiles.
Third, we develop our pipeline on explainable tree ensembles (Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM) to preserve compliance with the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA) and the Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA), avoiding black-box deep architectures.
Finally, we make our full experimental suite and an interactive decision demo publicly available for auditing.

\section{PRELIMINARIES AND PROBLEM SETUP}

\subsection{Split Conformal Classification}
We consider a standard supervised classification problem where transactions are represented by exchangeable tuples $Z = (X, A, Y) \sim \mathcal{D}$ over feature space $\mathcal{X} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$, sensitive demographic space $\mathcal{A} = \{0, 1\}$, and label space $\mathcal{Y} = \{0, 1, \dots, K-1\}$. In consumer lending, $K = 2$, with $Y=1$ denoting full loan repayment and $Y=0$ denoting credit default.

The available historical dataset is partitioned into three disjoint subsets: a training split $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$, a calibration split $\mathcal{D}_{\text{cal}} = \{(X_i, A_i, Y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$, and a test split $\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}$. A base probabilistic classifier $\hat{\pi}: \mathcal{X} \to \Delta^{K-1}$ is trained on $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$, outputting class probabilities $\hat{\pi}_k(x) = \hat{\mathbb{P}}(Y = k \mid X = x)$.

To evaluate the consistency between a proposed label $y$ and the model's estimate, we employ a nonconformity score function $S(x, y): \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y} \to \mathbb{R}$, where larger scores reflect lower model confidence in label $y$. We analyze two standard scoring rules:
First, the softmax nonconformity score, which measures the shortfall in predicted probability for candidate label $y$:
\begin{equation}
S_{\text{softmax}}(x, y) = 1 - \hat{\pi}_y(x).
\end{equation}
Second, the Adaptive Prediction Sets (APS) score \citep{romano2020classification}, which sums the predicted probabilities of classes ranked ahead of label $y$:
\begin{equation}
S_{\text{aps}}(x, y) = \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} \hat{\pi}_{(k)}(x) \cdot \mathbb{I}\left( \hat{\pi}_{(k)}(x) \ge \hat{\pi}_y(x) \right),
\end{equation}
where $(k)$ indexes classes ordered in descending probability mass.

Given calibration scores $s_i = S(X_i, Y_i)$ for $i=1, \dots, n$, the pooled empirical quantile at significance level $\alpha$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:quantile_def}
\hat{q}_{1-\alpha} = \text{Quantile}\left( \frac{\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil}{n};\; \{s_i\}_{i=1}^n \right).
\end{equation}
For any subsequent test transaction $X_{n+1}$, the valid prediction set is:
\begin{equation}
C(X_{n+1}) = \left\{ y \in \mathcal{Y} : S(X_{n+1}, y) \le \hat{q}_{1-\alpha} \right\}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Uncertainty Fairness Metrics}
In conformal inference, fairness cannot be measured purely through hard classification error. We evaluate performance across two operational criteria:

\begin{definition}[Group Coverage Gap]
Let $\text{Cov}_a(C) = \mathbb{P}(Y \in C(X) \mid A = a)$ denote the empirical coverage for group $a \in \mathcal{A}$. The coverage gap across demographic groups is:
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\text{cov}}(C) = \left| \text{Cov}_1(C) - \text{Cov}_0(C) \right|.
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Set-Size Disparity Ratio]
Let $|C(X)|$ denote the cardinality of the prediction set. The mean set size for group $a$ is $\bar{\mathcal{S}}_a(C) = \mathbb{E}[|C(X)| \mid A = a]$. The set-size disparity ratio is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{D}_{\text{size}}(C) = \frac{\max\left(\bar{\mathcal{S}}_0(C), \bar{\mathcal{S}}_1(C)\right)}{\min\left(\bar{\mathcal{S}}_0(C), \bar{\mathcal{S}}_1(C)\right)}.
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

A disparity ratio of $\mathcal{D}_{\text{size}}(C) = 1.0$ indicates perfect equity in prediction set cardinality. Ratios significantly exceeding $1.0$ indicate that applicants from one demographic group are subjected to disproportionate uncertainty.

\section{THE COVERAGE-EQUITY PARADOX}
\label{sec:paradox}

Why does naive group-conditional conformal calibration worsen administrative disparity in retail lending? We formalize the underlying mechanism below.

\subsection{Analytical Origin of Set-Size Inflation}
Consider a binary classification setting $\mathcal{Y} = \{0, 1\}$. Suppose group $A=0$ represents an underserved demographic group with sample size $n_0 \ll n_1$, while $A=1$ represents the majority demographic. In retail credit datasets, minority populations frequently exhibit thinner credit files, resulting in higher posterior variance in predicted probabilities.

Let $S(x, y) = 1 - \hat{\pi}_y(x)$. In group-conditional calibration, separate empirical quantiles $\hat{q}^{(0)}$ and $\hat{q}^{(1)}$ are computed over the respective calibration subsets $\{s_i : A_i = 0\}$ and $\{s_i : A_i = 1\}$.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:set_size}
For binary classification with softmax scoring $S(x, y) = 1 - \hat{\pi}_y(x)$, a test transaction $x$ receives an ambiguous two-element prediction set $C(x) = \{0, 1\}$ if and only if:
\begin{equation}
1 - \hat{q}^{(a)} \le \hat{\pi}_1(x) \le \hat{q}^{(a)}.
\end{equation}
The width of this ambiguous decision region is:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{W}\left(\hat{q}^{(a)}\right) = \max\left(0,\; 2\hat{q}^{(a)} - 1\right).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By definition, label 0 is included in $C(x)$ if and only if $1 - \hat{\pi}_0(x) \le \hat{q}^{(a)}$, which simplifies to $\hat{\pi}_1(x) \le \hat{q}^{(a)}$ since $\hat{\pi}_0(x) + \hat{\pi}_1(x) = 1$. Similarly, label 1 is included if and only if $1 - \hat{\pi}_1(x) \le \hat{q}^{(a)}$, which requires $\hat{\pi}_1(x) \ge 1 - \hat{q}^{(a)}$. Both labels belong to $C(x)$ simultaneously if and only if $1 - \hat{q}^{(a)} \le \hat{\pi}_1(x) \le \hat{q}^{(a)}$. The span of this interval is $\hat{q}^{(a)} - (1 - \hat{q}^{(a)}) = 2\hat{q}^{(a)} - 1$ whenever $\hat{q}^{(a)} \ge 0.5$, and 0 otherwise.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:set_size} reveals the direct mathematical driver of disparate impact: the ambiguous window $\mathcal{W}(\hat{q}^{(a)})$ expands monotonically as the calibration cutoff $\hat{q}^{(a)}$ increases. When an underserved demographic group exhibits greater variance in nonconformity scores, achieving the nominal $1-\alpha$ coverage target forces $\hat{q}^{(0)}$ substantially upward. This immediately expands the ambiguity band, trapping a higher fraction of minority applicants in the indecisive set $\{0, 1\}$.

\begin{remark}[Administrative Harm in Consumer Credit]
Under the Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA) and Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) guidelines, lenders cannot issue indeterminate outcomes. An applicant receiving $\{0, 1\}$ cannot receive automated clearance. Instead, they are flagged for secondary underwriting audits, requiring additional tax filings or employment documentation. This administrative burden depresses loan completion rates and exposes applicants to subjective human auditor bias \citep{charpentier2024insurance}.
\end{remark}

\section{THE FAIRTRANSCP FRAMEWORK}

To resolve the coverage-equity paradox, FairTransCP formulates calibration as a multi-objective optimization problem rather than an unconstrained per-group procedure.

\subsection{Dual-Objective Quantile Calibration}
Let $\hat{q}_{\text{pooled}}$ denote the pooled marginal threshold computed across all calibration samples, and let $\hat{q}^{(g)}$ denote the group-conditional quantile for group $g \in \mathcal{A}$.

FairTransCP defines the effective calibration threshold for group $g$ via a regularized convex combination:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:interpolation}
\tilde{q}^{(g)}(\lambda) = (1 - \lambda)\hat{q}^{(g)} + \lambda \hat{q}_{\text{pooled}}, \quad \lambda \in [0, 1].
\end{equation}

The regularizer $\lambda$ provides continuous operational control over the fairness-cardinality trade-off. Setting $\lambda = 0$ yields pure group-conditional conformal prediction, enforcing exact coverage equality at the expense of high set-size disparity. Conversely, setting $\lambda = 1$ recovers standard marginal conformal prediction, which equalizes decision cutoffs across groups and minimizes artificial set-size inflation, but permits coverage variation. For intermediate values $\lambda \in [0.3, 0.5]$, FairTransCP dampens extreme threshold excursions on noise-prone minority groups, curbing set-size explosion while preserving worst-group coverage within provable bounds.

\subsection{Theoretical Bounds on Regularized Coverage}
A key theoretical question is whether interpolating calibration thresholds compromises the distribution-free coverage guarantee. Theorem~\ref{thm:bound} establishes a finite-sample lower bound.

\begin{theorem}[Coverage Lower Bound]
\label{thm:bound}
Let $\hat{q}^{(a)}$ and $\hat{q}_{\text{pooled}}$ be the empirical quantiles for group $a$ and the pooled calibration set at significance level $1-\alpha$. For any regularizer $\lambda \in [0, 1]$, the interpolated threshold $\tilde{q}^{(a)}(\lambda) = (1 - \lambda)\hat{q}^{(a)} + \lambda \hat{q}_{\text{pooled}}$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{P}\left(S(X, Y) \le \tilde{q}^{(a)}(\lambda) \mid A=a\right) \ge (1-\alpha) - \lambda \cdot \delta_a,
\end{equation}
where $\delta_a = \mathbb{P}\left(S(X, Y) \in (\hat{q}_{\text{pooled}}, \hat{q}^{(a)}) \mid A=a\right)_+$, with $(\cdot)_+ = \max(0, \cdot)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By construction of the group-conditional empirical quantile, the base coverage satisfies $\mathbb{P}(S(X, Y) \le \hat{q}^{(a)} \mid A=a) \ge 1-\alpha$. The regularized threshold shifts by $\tilde{q}^{(a)} - \hat{q}^{(a)} = \lambda (\hat{q}_{\text{pooled}} - \hat{q}^{(a)})$. When $\hat{q}_{\text{pooled}} < \hat{q}^{(a)}$, the probability mass lost by lowering the threshold cannot exceed the mass residing in the inter-quantile region $(\hat{q}_{\text{pooled}}, \hat{q}^{(a)})$ under group distribution $\mathcal{D}_a$, scaled linearly by $\lambda$. Conversely, when $\hat{q}_{\text{pooled}} \ge \hat{q}^{(a)}$, the threshold shifts upward, and group coverage strictly increases above $1-\alpha$. Therefore, worst-case coverage degradation is bounded by $\lambda \delta_a$.
\end{proof}

Theorem~\ref{thm:bound} proves that FairTransCP does not induce uncontrolled coverage collapse; coverage loss is strictly bounded by the probability mass between the group and pooled quantiles.

\begin{algorithm}[tb]
\caption{FairTransCP Calibration and Prediction}
\label{alg:fairtranscp}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Calibration split $\mathcal{D}_{\text{cal}} = \{(X_i, A_i, Y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$, test transaction $X_{n+1}$ with sensitive attribute $A_{n+1} = a$, significance level $\alpha$, regularizer $\lambda \in [0, 1]$, nonconformity score function $S$.
\STATE Fit base classifier $\hat{\pi}$ on $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$.
\STATE Compute nonconformity scores $s_i = S(X_i, Y_i)$ for all $i \in \{1, \dots, n\}$.
\STATE Calculate pooled marginal quantile $\hat{q}_{\text{pooled}}$ via Eq.~(\ref{eq:quantile_def}).
\FOR{each demographic group $g \in \mathcal{A}$}
    \STATE Filter group calibration scores $\mathcal{S}_g = \{s_i : A_i = g\}$.
    \STATE Compute group empirical quantile $\hat{q}^{(g)}$ at level $1 - \alpha$.
    \STATE Compute regularized threshold:
    \begin{equation}
    \tilde{q}^{(g)} = (1 - \lambda) \hat{q}^{(g)} + \lambda \hat{q}_{\text{pooled}}.
    \end{equation}
\ENDFOR
\STATE Construct prediction set for $X_{n+1}$:
\begin{equation}
C_{\text{fair}}(X_{n+1}) = \left\{ y \in \mathcal{Y} : S(X_{n+1}, y) \le \tilde{q}^{(a)} \right\}.
\end{equation}
\IF{$C_{\text{fair}}(X_{n+1}) = \emptyset$}
    \STATE $C_{\text{fair}}(X_{n+1}) \leftarrow \{\arg\max_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} \hat{\pi}_y(X_{n+1})\}$.
\ENDIF
\RETURN $C_{\text{fair}}(X_{n+1})$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Selecting the Regularization Weight $\lambda^*$}
In production environments, the optimal regularizer $\lambda^*$ is selected on validation data by minimizing a joint loss:
\begin{equation}
\min_{\lambda \in [0, 1]} \; \Delta_{\text{cov}}(\tilde{q}(\lambda)) + \beta \cdot \mathcal{D}_{\text{size}}(\tilde{q}(\lambda))
\end{equation}
subject to $\text{Cov}_a \ge 1 - \alpha - \epsilon$ for all $a \in \mathcal{A}$, where $\beta > 0$ weights the cost of set-size disparity and $\epsilon$ denotes allowable slack. Across our benchmarks, grid search over $\lambda \in [0.2, 0.5]$ consistently identifies the Pareto knee.

\section{EXPERIMENTAL EVALUATION}

\subsection{Experimental Design}
We evaluate our methodology on three financial and demographic benchmarks from the UCI Machine Learning Repository. German Credit ($N=1{,}000$, 20 attributes) evaluates retail loan risk, where applicant age ($<25$ years vs.\ $\ge 25$ years) serves as the protected feature \citep{hofmann1994german}. Taiwan Credit Default ($N=30{,}000$, 23 attributes) tracks credit card defaults, with client sex treated as sensitive \citep{yeh2009comparisons}. Adult Census Income ($N=48{,}842$, 14 attributes) provides a large-scale demographic benchmark predicting income thresholds ($>50\text{K}$), with sex evaluated as the protected attribute \citep{kohavi1996scaling}.

To ensure compliance with banking auditability standards, we deploy three classical tree ensembles: Random Forest \citep{breiman2001random} (100 trees, maximum depth 8), XGBoost \citep{chen2016xgboost} (100 estimators, learning rate 0.1), and LightGBM \citep{ke2017lightgbm}. Tree-based models are preferred in credit scoring because financial regulators demand clear feature attribution and monotonic decision boundaries.

For each experimental run, records are split into 50\% training, 25\% calibration, and 25\% testing. All trials are repeated across 5 independent random seeds ($n_{\text{trials}}=5$). The nominal miscoverage level is configured to $\alpha = 0.10$, representing a target coverage of 90\%.

\subsection{Empirical Analysis}
Table~\ref{tab:main_results} presents empirical metrics across all benchmark configurations. The results highlight three main findings.

First, the experiments confirm the severity of the disparity spike under Mondrian CP. On Adult Income with XGBoost, Standard CP achieves a balanced set-size disparity of $1.040 \pm 0.004$. When Mondrian CP is enforced to equalize coverage across demographic groups, the disparity ratio jumps to $\mathbf{1.637 \pm 0.055}$---an inflation of nearly 60\%. On German Credit with Random Forest, Mondrian CP similarly increases disparity from $1.032$ to $1.059$. This confirms that group-conditional calibration systematically widens prediction sets for protected demographic segments that exhibit higher epistemic uncertainty.

Second, FairTransCP eliminates this disparity surge while maintaining reliable coverage. On Adult Income (XGBoost), FairTransCP compresses the disparity ratio from $1.637$ back to $\mathbf{1.040 \pm 0.004}$, while preserving worst-group coverage at $0.953 \pm 0.003$ (well above the $0.90$ nominal target). In contrast, while the LC-CP baseline reduces disparity, its worst-group coverage falls to $0.894 \pm 0.005$, breaching the safety threshold. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}, FairTransCP dominates alternative fair baselines along the coverage-disparity Pareto frontier.

Third, our analysis exposes the failure mode of trivial fair predictors. The Generic Fair CP baseline records an ostensibly perfect set-size disparity of $1.000 \pm 0.000$ and complete coverage. However, inspection of set cardinality reveals that it achieves this by predicting $\{0, 1\}$ for virtually all applicants ($\bar{\mathcal{S}} = 1.995$ to $2.000$). In consumer lending, outputting ambiguous predictions for every applicant renders the model useless for risk management. FairTransCP preserves informative set sizes ($\bar{\mathcal{S}} \approx 1.65$) while enforcing cardinality parity.

\section{LEGAL AND REGULATORY RELEVANCE}
Automated credit decisions are governed by strict fair lending statutes in both the United States and the European Union. Under the Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA) and Regulation B, underwriting workflows are prohibited from generating unjustified disparate impact against protected classes. Even when models exclude explicit demographic features from training data, systematically issuing larger, more ambiguous prediction sets to minority borrowers creates administrative barriers that constitute actionable disparate impact.

Furthermore, the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA) mandates that lenders provide Adverse Action notices detailing specific explanatory reasons whenever an applicant is rejected or delayed. An ambiguous conformal prediction $\{0, 1\}$ provides no clear reason for denial, creating legal compliance vulnerability for lending institutions. Finally, the EU AI Act categorizes automated credit evaluation as a high-risk AI application under Article 14, requiring verifiable uncertainty calibration and human-in-the-loop oversight. FairTransCP provides an audit-compliant methodology that meets uncertainty requirements without generating operational bottlenecks for protected groups.

\section{CONCLUSION}
In this work, we analyzed the coverage-equity paradox in conformal classification: enforcing strict demographic coverage parity inflates prediction set sizes for vulnerable demographic groups, converting classification error into administrative delays. We introduced FairTransCP, a regularized calibration framework that balances coverage equity and set-size parity simultaneously. Supported by finite-sample theoretical bounds and empirical benchmarks across German Credit, Taiwan Default, and Adult Census datasets, FairTransCP demonstrates that financial institutions can deploy reliable distribution-free uncertainty guarantees while upholding demographic equity in automated decision workflows.
